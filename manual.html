Training Manual

Four skills, taught in small rungs: settle on his bed from anywhere, stay quiet when someone knocks, wait to be greeted, and walk at your side. Work the three sessions on the Today tab and move up a rung only when he passes.

House rules

Two people train him, so the words and the rules have to match. A cue that means one thing with you and another with your partner means nothing to him.

How to run any session

  1. Five reps, then decide. Four or five right: make it slightly harder. Three: repeat as is. Two or fewer: make it easier. The tracker on the Today tab does this count for you.
  2. Change one thing at a time. Longer, or further away, or more distracting. When you raise one, drop the other two.
  3. One cue, once. If he doesn't respond, the step is too hard. Repeating the word teaches him the first one doesn't count.
  4. Pay where you want him. Bed treats go on the bed between his front paws. Heel treats arrive at your left trouser seam.
  5. Stop while he still wants more. Five minutes is plenty. End on a rep he got right.
  6. Pea-sized treats. Use part of his daily kibble for easy reps at home and chicken, cheese or liver for doors, guests and outdoors. Take the calories out of his dinner.

What “firm” means here

  • The rule is the same every day and with every person. He never gets greeted for rushing a guest, not even once by a visitor who “doesn't mind”.
  • You set up the house so he can't practise the old habit: leash on or gate closed before guests come in, treats by the door before the courier arrives.
  • Good things come through you: the door opens, the guest says hello, the walk moves forward, when he does the calm thing.
  • A mistake costs him the reward and nothing more. No leash pops, scruffing, shouting or pinning.

Why this works

You said you're fine with being firm and establishing yourselves as the alphas, as long as it's what the research supports. The research supports the firm part and not the alpha part.

The alpha idea came from captive wolves and was withdrawn by the scientist who popularised it. Early studies watched unrelated wolves forced together in enclosures. Wild packs turned out to be families led by parents, and David Mech, whose 1970 book spread the term, has spent decades correcting it. Studies of dogs find that “dominance” describes specific relationships over specific resources. It does not explain barking at the door, crowding guests or pulling on a leash. A dog does those things because they have worked for him.

Methods built on intimidation perform no better and carry real costs. Reviews and controlled studies find that dogs trained with leash corrections, shouting or physical confrontation show more stress behaviours, higher cortisol and a more pessimistic outlook, and owners who use confrontational techniques report more aggressive responses. Where reward-based and aversive methods have been compared directly, the reward-based groups learned at least as well. That is why the American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior recommends reward-based methods for all training and behaviour problems.

What does work is controlling consequences. Behaviour that pays gets repeated; behaviour that stops paying fades. Every rung in this manual does three things: prevents the old habit from paying, teaches a replacement he can't do at the same time (a dog lying on his bed can't be at the door), and pays that replacement heavily until it is his default.

About the slip collar

You've already seen that it doesn't stop him. A dog pulling into a slip lead isn't learning anything, and sustained pressure on the neck raises pressure inside the eye and loads the windpipe. Walk him on a well-fitted Y-front harness with a front chest clip. It doesn't train him by itself either, but it turns him slightly toward you when he pulls and takes his throat out of it while the Heel ladder does the teaching.

Exercise comes first

Under 30 minutes a day is low for a three-year-old standard poodle, an athletic retrieving breed. Most adult standards need an hour or more of activity, some of it real running, plus work for their brain. A dog with unspent energy finds settling on a bed much harder. Building this up will likely help every skill here more than any single exercise.

How long this takes

With daily practice, expect a reliable “get cozy” from other rooms in roughly four to six weeks, calm door and guest routines in six to ten, and a dependable loose leash in two to three months. Those are typical ranges, not promises. He is three, so the old habits have had years of practice.

When to call a professional

Everything here assumes what you described: friendly and over-excited. If you ever see stiffening, growling, a hard stare, snapping, or him hiding from visitors, stop the guest exercises and book a certified behaviour consultant or a veterinary behaviourist. See your vet too if the barking or restlessness is new.
